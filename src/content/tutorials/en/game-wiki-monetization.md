## Where does the money in a game wiki actually come from?

Three sources, in the order they become available:

1. **Display advertising** — the workhorse. Guide and codes traffic is high-volume and ad-friendly; an ad network pays per thousand views. This starts paying at modest traffic levels and scales with content.
2. **Affiliate commissions** — links to games, storefronts and gear. Smaller but useful, and strongest on pages where the reader is one click from a purchase (game pages, "is it worth buying" guides, setup-gear lists).
3. **Sponsorships and community support** — once the site is a known fixture for a game, publishers may pay for placement and readers may chip in. This is a consequence of authority, not a strategy to start with.

That order is specific to game wikis: game and gear affiliate commissions run thin, so display advertising earns the workhorse role. General content sites (SaaS tools, tutorial libraries) flip it — affiliate recommendations come first there. The template's demo wiki carries that other order in its [monetization tutorial](/guides/monetize-site-tutorial/).

What does not work on day one: ads on a site with no visitors (networks will not even approve it), affiliate links with no traffic, and donation buttons with no community. Every failed wiki-monetization story is some order of these three applied too early.

## Stage 0 — traffic before anything

Monetization is a function of visitors, so the first milestone is content that earns visits: 10–15 genuinely useful pages answering real searches (boss guides, codes pages, tier lists), published, indexed, and refreshed as the game patches. The honest timeline is weeks of steady publishing before numbers justify any revenue work — and the freshness loop that keeps pages current is part of the product, not admin. The [weekly ops lesson](/landing/docs/weekly-ops/) is that loop as a 30-minute routine.

## Stage 1 — display advertising

With steady traffic, apply to an ad network. Google AdSense is the realistic first network for a new site: it approves modest sites, and a template with ad slots built in (lazy-loaded, consent-gated, layout-safe) means turning revenue on is a configuration change, not a redesign. Bigger gaming-focused networks pay better but require traffic thresholds that a new site does not meet yet — they are the upgrade path a year in, not the entry point.

Two placement rules that protect the traffic you monetize: ads must not push content around while loading (that layout shift is measured, and readers hate it), and codes/steps must stay visible above whatever advertising surrounds them. The [ads lesson](/landing/docs/enable-ads/) covers networks, placement and the application checklist in detail.

## Stage 2 — affiliates on buying-intent pages

Add affiliate links where the reader is deciding, not just reading: the game's store page on your "is it worth it" guide, the capture card on your streaming-setup page. Keep disclosure visible — labeled affiliate links are both the legal norm and the trust-preserving one. The failure mode to avoid is carpet-bombing every page with links; a wiki lives on being a trusted reference, and trust converts better than link density.

## The fork in the road that decides what all of this pays: who hosts you

The same wiki traffic pays very differently depending on where the site lives:

- **Platform-hosted** (the big wiki farms): they handle everything, but they also run the advertising and keep the revenue split — the contributor's cut of a monetized wiki platform is a fraction of what the same traffic earns directly, and the platform's domain means the site's authority never quite belongs to you.
- **Your own domain**: you run the ads, you sign the network contracts, the revenue (and the domain's growing search authority) is yours. The trade is that you own the ops — which a static, serverless template reduces to nearly nothing.

This is the honest core of the [comparison page](/landing/comparison/): for big community encyclopedias, a hosted platform is fine. For an individual or a small team trying to earn from a game site, the platform's revenue split is the whole business model working against you.

## What the staged path looks like on a calendar

- **Weeks 1–2**: publish the first 10–15 pages; submit to search engines; set up analytics so "traffic is real" is a number, not a feeling ([indexing playbook](/landing/tutorials/get-indexed-by-google/)).
- **Weeks 3–8**: keep the freshness loop; watch which pages pull searches; write more of what works.
- **When traffic is consistent**: apply to AdSense; wire the pre-built slots; pass review.
- **Later**: layer affiliates on buying-intent pages; explore sponsorships once the site is a fixture.

Revenue follows traffic the way shade follows a tree — plant the content, water it weekly, and the monetization steps are configuration, not courage.
